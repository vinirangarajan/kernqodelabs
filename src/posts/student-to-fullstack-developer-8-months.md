## Starting Point

*This is the story of Arjun, a junior developer on the KernQode Labs team, told in his own words.*

---

I graduated with a Computer Science degree from a mid-tier college in Coimbatore in May 2024. My CGPA was decent — 7.8 — but when I started applying for jobs, I realised I couldn't build a single thing on my own.

I knew C++ from college. I'd written some Java. But if you put a blank code editor in front of me and asked me to build a working login system? I would have stared at it for an hour and then opened YouTube.

That was the gap. College taught me *about* programming. It didn't teach me to *program*.

## The Decision

I refused to apply for another off-campus drive where 2,000 students compete for 20 seats. I gave myself 8 months. If I couldn't get a real job by then, I'd reconsider.

I opened a notebook and wrote down one goal: **Build three projects that actually work and that I'm proud to show.**

Not certificates. Not courses completed. Not LinkedIn skills listed. *Projects.*

## Month 1–2: Learning to Learn

I picked one resource and stuck to it — the **Odin Project** for web development fundamentals. No jumping between courses. Every time I felt lost, I resisted the urge to switch resources and instead re-read what I didn't understand.

The biggest mindset shift: **stop watching, start building.** I had a habit of watching tutorial videos and feeling like I was learning. But the moment I closed the video and tried to do it myself — blank. The muscle memory isn't in watching. It's in doing.

Rule I set for myself: for every 1 hour of tutorial, I had to spend 2 hours building something from scratch, using only documentation.

## Month 3–4: First Real Project

I built a **task manager web app** using HTML, CSS, vanilla JavaScript. Nothing fancy. But every feature worked:

- Add tasks with due dates
- Mark complete, delete
- Filter by status
- Data persisted in `localStorage`

It took me 3 weeks. I rebuilt it twice because the first version was spaghetti code I couldn't read a week later. The second rebuild was cleaner — and rebuilding it taught me more than the first build.

I put it on GitHub. Then I shared it in a developer Discord. A stranger replied: *"This is clean, well-structured. Nice work."*

I don't think I slept that night.

## Month 5–6: React and Backend

I learned React from the official documentation — not a YouTube course. Painful, but it forced me to actually read carefully.

For the backend, I used Node.js + Express. I built a small REST API for a library management system: add books, track who borrowed what, simple authentication.

Mistake I made: I spent 2 weeks trying to make it "perfect" before showing anyone. That's called **over-engineering out of fear**. My mentor at the time told me: ship it ugly and get feedback. The feedback will make it better. Keeping it private just delays your learning.

## Month 7: The Project That Got Me Hired

I built a **freelancer invoicing tool** — a web app where a freelancer can create clients, add services, generate PDF invoices, and track payment status.

Why this project? Because I was frustrated that existing tools were either too expensive or too complicated for someone just starting out. I built the tool I actually wanted.

This project had:
- React frontend
- Node.js + Express backend
- PostgreSQL database (deployed on Railway)
- PDF generation with `pdfkit`
- Deployed live on Vercel + Railway

When I showed it to KernQode Labs in my application, they asked me to walk them through the codebase on a call. I could answer every question about every decision I made — because I'd made every decision myself.

That's the interview. Not a coding test. Not an algorithm puzzle. A conversation about something real I built.

## Month 8: First Day

I joined KernQode Labs as a junior developer. My first week was overwhelming — real codebases are messier than personal projects, deployment pipelines are complicated, and working with a team requires communication skills nobody teaches you.

But the fundamentals were solid. And fundamentals are what let you learn everything else.

## What I'd Tell Myself at Month 0

1. **Pick one path and go deep.** Don't learn React, Vue, Angular, Svelte simultaneously. Pick one, master it, then explore.

2. **Build in public.** Share your ugly first projects. The feedback is gold and the accountability keeps you moving.

3. **Read the docs.** Not as a last resort — as a first resort. Official documentation is almost always better than random blog posts.

4. **Find one person ahead of you who will give honest feedback.** Not cheerleaders. Someone who will tell you your code is bad and why.

5. **Track what you build, not what you watch.** Your portfolio is projects, not courses.

The journey is not linear. There will be weeks where you feel like you're moving backwards. That's normal — it means you've learned enough to see your own gaps.

Keep going.
